### Notes

- The non-affricate aspirated stops are variably affricated in Haltzeonian dialects; this is particularly the case for stressed aspirated stops.
- Plain stops are usually slack voiced [b̥ d̥ d̥͡ʒ̊ g̊ ɢ̥], but this is somewhat variable, and the primary way these are distinguished is aspiration (or a lack thereof).
    - The plain uvular stop has other allophones in certain dialects - namely [ʁ̊ ʀ̆].

In Haltzeonian, all vowels can technically be nasalized. However, nasalized /ɛ ə ɔ/ has the same pronunciation as nasalized /e ɐ o/. Regardless, these vowels come primarily from historic coda /ŋ/, though high vowels /i e/ nasalize when followed by /ɲ/.
## Vowel harmony
The vowels of Haltzeonian can be changed as follows:

| standard   | fronted         | backed          |
|------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| i [i]      | ---             | ui [ɯ]          |
| u [u]      | iu [y]          | ---             |
| e ĩ [e ĩ]  | ---             | eo uĩ [ə ɯ̃]    |
| o ũ [o ũ]  | eo iũ [ə ỹ]     | ---             |
| ae ẽ [ɛ ẽ] | ye yẽ [jɛ j̃ɛ̃] | ue uẽ [ø ø̃]    |
| eo [ə]     | yeo/yae [ɛ]     | weo/wao [ɔ]     |
| ao õ [ɔ õ] | io iõ [ʌ ʌ̃]    | wo wõ [wɑ w̃ɑ̃] |
| a ã [ɐ ɐ̃] | ---             | ---             |
Every vowel after the stressed one follows the same backness position. If the first vowel in the word is a stressed /i e jV/, then the entire word is fronted. If the first vowel in the word is a stressed /u o wV/, then the entire word is backed. If there is not initial stress, then all syllables before the stressed one are standard.

/ɛ ə ɔ ɐ/ are neutral in the sense that they do not trigger harmonization. However, they do trigger harmonization if /jV wV/ are found. An on-glide of the opposite harmony becomes [ɥ], e.g. míkwo [ˈmikɥɑ].

## Stress
Stress is complex in Kardonian, but generally not phonemic. It relies on "syllable weight", where a syllable which has a stop coda is very heavy. Closed syllables that do not end in a stop are heavy, open syllables with a nasal vowel are light, and open syllables with an oral vowel are very light. Syllables with two onset consonants go up one place on the weight hierarchy unless they already have a coda stop. The first syllable is the only one which can be onsetless, and it goes down one place on the weight hierarchy unless it already is open and oral.

If the word is bisyllabic, then the heaviest syllable is stressed. If both syllables have the same weight, the first syllable receives stress unless its vowel is open-mid oral _ae, eo, ao_ and there is no onset. A geminated stop, such as in the word _mekka_, is counted as a stop to each syllable.

For trisyllabic words, the first syllable receives primary stress if and only if it is closed and both following syllables are open. If this is not the case, then the stress rules apply to the last two syllables as if they were their own word. For words with more syllables than this, primary stress is determined as if the last three syllables were their own word.

Words with 2 or more syllables before the determined final stress location will receive secondary stress. This is determined the same way as prior, as if the last stressed syllable and all syllables after it were removed. This continues until the beginning of the word is reached. For example, the name _Albaqaerqe_ has penultimate stress, thus the _al_ is also stressed: [ˌɐʟpɐˈqʰɛrqʰe].

Monosyllabic affixes do not change the stress position unless they've been lexicalized. This does mean that, for example, the word for money, _lhõnanheon_, is pronounced [ˌʎɔ̃nɐˈɲən], even though the identical word for a few lhõnan is pronounced [ʎɔ̃ˈnɐɲən]. This is the only case where stress is phonemic, and even then, these affixes are generally considered a separate morpheme. Under this analysis, the stress within the morpheme _lhõnan_ does not change; it is only once lexicalized into a single morphene _lhõnanheon_ that it changes.
